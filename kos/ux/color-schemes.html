UX — Visual Identity

Palette Specimen

Five color-theory candidates for the Friendster Rebuild's visual identity — a text-first, follow-based community built around one payoff: feeling noticed when someone you know responds. Each palette below picks a different harmony (analogous, complementary, split-complementary, triadic, monochromatic) and carries it through a real nav bar, post card, and follow/notification moment so it can be judged in context, not as loose swatches.

Project: soc-med-clone Stack: Rails 8 · Tailwind Stage: pre-build UX pass

Every option deliberately avoids the default social-network blue — it's the one color every network in this space already reaches for, and the brief calls for something that reads as its own thing from the first screen. Hex values below are computed from the stated HSL, and the contrast ratios are measured, not eyeballed — text on a filled button uses whichever of white/dark ink actually clears WCAG AA, matching the convention already set for this studio's other projects.

Analogous — 16° / 38° / 350°

Warm Signal

Three neighboring hues on the wheel — coral, amber, rose — kept within a 70° arc. Nothing here fights anything else, which suits a feed meant to be sat in and scrolled, not alarmed by. Coral carries the brand and primary actions; amber breaks in for secondary buttons and badges; rose is reserved for the follow/notification moment, close enough in temperature to feel like part of the family rather than a jarring alert color.

Complementary — 225° ↔ 45°

Indigo & Ember

A true pair straight across the wheel. Indigo is the everyday surface — calm enough to read a feed against for a while — and it has exactly one job outside that: carry the follow/notification moment in amber, the one color in this palette indigo has no relationship to. That's the point of picking complements here — the "someone noticed you" moment gets a color nothing else on the page is allowed to use.

Split-complementary — 178° base / 332° · 28°

Retro Teal

Teal anchors the brand, then instead of jumping straight to its complement (a plain coral-red), the two hues flanking that complement do the work — magenta and orange. It's the same family of teal-plus-warm-accent that early web social networks leaned on, without literally reusing anyone's brand colors, and it gives three distinct roles instead of two: teal for the everyday chrome, magenta for anything "premium" or personal (profile accents), orange for the single loudest action on screen.

Triadic — 265° / 145° / 25°

Recess

The one harmony not tried yet: three hues spaced evenly around the wheel instead of clustered or opposed. Violet, green, and orange, each 120° apart — the most playful option of the five, closer to a friend-group's mixed energy than a single brand mood. Violet carries the brand and everyday chrome, green is the follower/growth signal, orange is reserved for the one loudest action on any given screen. The trade with a triad this vivid is discipline — it reads as fun exactly as long as no fourth color sneaks in.

Monochromatic — 315°, tonal only

Single Thread

32% L46% L56% L

No second or third hue at all — one plum, carried from a near-black shade up to a pale tint, with role built entirely from lightness and saturation instead of hue contrast. The most editorial and the calmest of the five: nothing on screen competes with anything else for attention by color alone, which suits a text-first app that wants the words to be the loudest thing on the page. The honest trade: the follow/notification moment can't lean on a hue jump to grab the eye — it has to earn attention with saturation, weight, or motion instead.

Lean

Retro Teal is still the strongest fit on brief. This is a Friendster rebuild — a nod to that era of the social web is on-theme in a way most of the others aren't, and teal-plus-warm-accent gives Secondary a real job (profile identity, not just "the other button color") that a tightly-clustered analogous set can't support as cleanly.

Indigo & Ember is the safer pick if the "feel noticed" moment should read as unmistakably separate from everything else on screen — true complements give that contrast no other harmony here can match, at the cost of the retro identity.

Recess is the one worth a second look if the product should feel more like a group of friends than a brand — it's the most vivid option here, and the most demanding to keep disciplined as the app grows past five UI colors. Single Thread is the opposite bet: the most restrained and text-first of the five, at the cost of a notification moment that has to work harder than a hue jump to get noticed.

All five pass AA on every text/fill pairing actually used above. Once one is chosen, it graduates to a kos/decisions/ux/color-scheme.md decision file — matching how bus-reservation recorded its own palette call, dark-mode variant included at that stage, not this one.
